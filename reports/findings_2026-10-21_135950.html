01

Booked-but-unused slots ('no-shows' or 'ghost bookings') block others from shared instruments, and users rely on manual workarounds

4 of 6 participants

Supported by P01, P02, P04, P06

Evidence What participants said

P01 described weekly no-shows on the confocal. Other people see the slot as taken and give up. P01 now walks past the room to check whether anyone is there, asked for a check-in that releases the slot after fifteen minutes, and named this as the one thing to fix. P02 said the two-photon often shows as booked but is empty, and admitted to booking 'just in case' because time is hard to get, calling it a vicious cycle. P04, who runs a core facility serving about thirty labs, said ghost bookings are worse there because labs don't know each other. P04 sends weekly reminder emails that help 'for about two days' and asked for a check-in or a way to see which labs no-show most. P06 said the scope is often empty when booked but, as the most junior person, does not feel able to use the empty slot.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

P03 The PI is less worried about unused bookings. P03 sees some slack as necessary because experiments run long or fail, and thinks auto-cancelling would add stress and hurt students who are running late.

P05 In a six-person startup, unused bookings are handled informally by talking across the bench, though P05 acknowledged it could differ in large shared facilities.

Interpretation What it might mean

The problem may grow with the number of users and how socially distant they are. It looks most acute in shared core facilities and busy university labs, and negligible in small co-located teams. Scarcity may drive defensive over-booking, which makes scarcity worse. The cost may fall unevenly on junior users who don't feel entitled to claim empty slots. A hard auto-release could meet resistance from PIs, so softer or configurable options may be worth exploring, such as grace periods, per-instrument settings, no-show visibility for facility managers, or prompts to release slots.

02

Notifications are too noisy to be useful, leading users to mute or filter them and miss critical messages

3 of 6 participants

Supported by P02, P05, P06

Evidence What participants said

P02 said they get an email whenever anyone changes anything, including on machines they never use. P02 turned all notifications off, then missed a maintenance cancellation of their own slot, lost an evening, and lost a week of sample prep. P05 receives a daily summary plus an email per change and couldn't work out how to keep one without the other. P05 now filters all of them into a folder they never look at. P06 gets many emails, doesn't really read them, and thinks most are about other people's bookings.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

P01 The lab manager values notifications highly. P01 wants to hear about any booking change on instruments they're responsible for and prefers too many to too few, while noting that others in the lab mute them.

Interpretation What it might mean

Notification needs may differ by role. Managers and instrument owners want broad change alerts, while bookers mainly need alerts about their own bookings. A single all-or-nothing setting may push bookers to opt out entirely, so high-stakes messages like cancellations of their own slots get lost. Role-based defaults, finer controls, or a separate priority channel for changes to one's own bookings might help.

03

Booking on a phone is fiddly and error-prone for users who are rarely at a desk

2 of 6 participants

Supported by P02, P06

Evidence What participants said

P02 books mostly from their phone, at the bench, between buildings, or on the bus. P02 described the mobile site as the desktop site shrunk down. P02 has booked the wrong hour twice due to mis-taps and then had to fix it on a laptop, and named mobile as the one thing to change. P06 books mostly on their phone between classes and said the slots are tiny and the page doesn't fit the screen.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

P05 P05 books on their phone without trouble and suggested their large phone may help, while noting the experience isn't polished.

Interpretation What it might mean

Mobile may be the primary booking context for students and other mobile bench workers. The non-responsive layout could cause real booking errors, and screen size may affect how severe the problem feels. A touch-friendly responsive booking flow could reduce mis-bookings.

04

Lack of calendar integration forces users to check two places or re-enter bookings by hand

4 of 6 participants

Supported by P01, P02, P04, P05

Evidence What participants said

P01 uses Outlook and has to check LabLoop and the calendar separately. P02 types bookings into Google Calendar by hand to avoid clashing with seminars. P04 wants the core's maintenance schedule to appear automatically in Outlook. P05 said everyone at their company uses Google Calendar, described LabLoop as 'a separate world', and named calendar sync as the one thing to change.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

P03 The PI said calendar integration doesn't matter much to them personally because they don't book, though they can see why students would want it.

Interpretation What it might mean

Calendar sync seems to be valued by those who book or manage schedules, and is lower priority for decision-makers who mainly read reports. Both Outlook and Google Calendar were mentioned, suggesting support for both may be needed. This may be a small daily friction rather than an acute pain, though it could also contribute to self-double-booking and missed slots.

05

New users hit an unexplained 'not authorised' block with no guidance on training or who to contact

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by P06

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

P06 was onboarded only with a link. On the first booking attempt the scope showed 'not authorised' with no reason or contact. P06 assumed their account was broken, learned from a grad student that training was probably required, and took about a week to resolve it. During that week P06 could not do the work they were hired for and felt embarrassed. P06 asked for even a single sentence explaining what to do.

Interpretation What it might mean

The training-gating that facility staff value (see related finding) may create a dead end for new users when the reason for denial isn't surfaced. Explaining the training requirement in the error and naming a contact could be a low-effort change that improves first-use experience and reduces lost time.

06

Near-simultaneous bookings can create double bookings that damage trust in the system

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by P04

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

P04 reported two double bookings on the cell sorter in the past month, where two people booked at almost the same moment and both showed up. P04 had to choose who got the slot and felt it made the facility look disorganised and themselves look biased. P04 named this as the one thing to change.

Interpretation What it might mean

This sounds like a possible concurrency issue in booking confirmation. It is single-source and described as rare, but it may matter disproportionately because it undermines the core promise of a booking system. High-traffic shared facilities may be most exposed. It may warrant technical investigation regardless of how often it is reported.

07

Usage and cost reporting requires manual cleanup and cannot reliably split costs by grant

2 of 6 participants

Supported by P01, P03

Evidence What participants said

P01 pulls a quarterly usage report for the PI and has to clean up the export in Excel first. P03 uses utilization data for grants and service contract negotiations and said the lab manager spends half a day reformatting the export. P03 explained that grant codes are entered in a free-text field that is often left blank or spelled inconsistently. P03 named proper cost reporting by grant as the one thing to change and said it is what justifies the subscription to the department.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

P05 For a small startup, department-billing features are seen as unnecessary overhead rather than a need.

Interpretation What it might mean

For university buyers, reporting may be closely tied to renewal decisions, since it is how PIs justify the cost. Structured grant-code entry, for example a validated picklist, plus budget-ready exports might reduce hidden admin labour. This need appears specific to academic and grant-funded customers.

08

Training-based booking permissions are highly valued where used, and wanted where not visible

2 of 6 participants

Supported by P03, P04

Evidence What participants said

P04 described restricting the cell sorter to trained users as critical, because the instrument is expensive and easy to break. Before this, untrained people would book it. P03 asked for instruments to be bookable only by trained people, visible in one place, instead of the lab manager keeping a separate list.

Interpretation What it might mean

P04 describes training gating as an existing capability, while P03 asks for it as if it were missing. This could point to a discoverability or configuration gap rather than a missing feature, though that is unconfirmed. Combined with P06's experience, the permission system may work well for administrators but poorly communicate its rules to the people affected.

09

Maintenance blocking is a well-liked feature for instrument owners

2 of 6 participants

Supported by P01, P04

Evidence What participants said

P01 said marking an instrument as down greys it out for everyone and works well. P04 called recurring maintenance windows their favourite feature, because they block themselves automatically and avoid disputes about unavailable times.

Interpretation What it might mean

Maintenance blocking appears to be a strength worth preserving. It could also be a natural anchor for calendar integration, which P04 specifically wanted for maintenance schedules. Note that P02's lost evening arose from a maintenance change they didn't see, which suggests the feature's value depends on reliable delivery of the related notification.

10

Small teams feel they are paying for enterprise features they don't need

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by P05

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

P05, at a six-scientist startup, said setup was quick and the product 'does what it says'. P05 asked for simpler pricing for small teams, citing department billing as an example of an unused university-oriented feature.

Interpretation What it might mean

This is a single source, but it may indicate a pricing or packaging fit gap for small commercial labs. Their needs (calendar sync, notification control) differ from those of university buyers (grant reporting, permissions). Tiered packaging could be worth exploring.
