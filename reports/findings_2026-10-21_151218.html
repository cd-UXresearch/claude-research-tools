Findings

01

The "not authorised" message gives no reason or next step, so new users get stuck and email the wrong people

3 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O04, O06

Evidence What participants said

O01 saw "You are not authorised to book this resource" on every instrument with no reason given. O01 thought the account was broken and emailed the university IT helpdesk, which said LabLoop was not theirs, before the lab manager explained the training requirements. O01 asked for the message to say why and what to do. O04, the training coordinator for a shared core, said new users are confused by the message: some email O04, some email IT, and some ask a friend to book for them. O04 wants the message to link to training sign-up. O06, a department admin, receives many emails from confused new users who cannot book. O06 forwards them to lab managers or the core because O06's name is on the welcome email.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

O02 Lab manager thinks it is fine that LabLoop does not explain access refusals. O02 sees onboarding as the lab's job and worries that a generic message could misdirect people away from the lab manager.

O05 Saw the message once but was not confused, because a lab-provided document had already explained the training requirement.

Interpretation What it might mean

The unexplained refusal may be generating misrouted support load across IT, admins, and core staff, as well as delays for new users. The disagreement suggests that a single generic message could clash with lab-specific processes. A configurable message, where each lab or core sets the reason, contact, or sign-up link, might satisfy both the core's need to scale and lab managers' wish to route people to a human.

02

Granting instrument access after training is manual, instrument-by-instrument, and error-prone

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O02, O04

Evidence What participants said

O02 grants permissions one instrument at a time after training. This takes about twenty minutes per person, and when O02 forgets one, a user later finds they cannot book. O02 tracks training completion in a separate spreadsheet and asked for roles or bundles that grant and revoke permissions in one go. O04 said training records live in the university learning system, which LabLoop does not connect to, so O04 manually unlocks users after each session. This takes an hour or two per training day, and nobody gets unlocked when O04 is away. O04 once unlocked the wrong person with a similar name and reported that both untrained access and wrongly blocked trained users happen.

Interpretation What it might mean

The manual permission model may be a root cause of both the admin burden and some of the new-user access problems, including delays, missed permissions, and wrong unlocks. Role-based permission bundles and an integration with training records could reduce workload and errors. The wrong-person unlock suggests this also has a safety dimension, not just an efficiency one.

03

Accounts of people who have left keep their permissions, including on dangerous instruments

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O02, O06

Evidence What participants said

O02 found someone who had left two years earlier and could still book the ultracentrifuge. O02 described feeling alarmed because the machine is dangerous for untrained users. O06 is not told when people leave, so their accounts stay active. O06 does a yearly clean-up that O06 described as guesswork.

Interpretation What it might mean

Lack of offboarding support may create safety and compliance risk, not only clutter. Account end dates, bulk revocation by role, or periodic review prompts could address this. The same end-date mechanism might also serve visitors (see O03).

04

Access barriers push new users into booking under other people's names

3 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O03, O04

Evidence What participants said

While waiting for confocal training, O01 borrowed a second-year student's time on the machine and found this awkward. O03's host booked time under her own name while O03 had no account. As a result, others thought the host was using the microscope constantly, and problems appeared to be from her sessions. O04 reported that some new users who hit the "not authorised" message ask a friend to book for them.

Interpretation What it might mean

Proxy bookings may undermine the accuracy of usage records, accountability for incidents, and possibly billing. They may also let untrained people onto instruments under someone else's name. Reducing time-to-access, or offering a sanctioned way to book on behalf of a trainee or guest, might reduce this behaviour.

05

Training availability and the lack of recognition for prior training delay new users' research

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O03

Evidence What participants said

O01 got centrifuge access in two days but waited almost three weeks for the confocal, because sessions run twice a month and the first was full. This held up O01's first rotation project. O03 had already completed the same training on the same microscope model at their home institution, but there was no way to record it, so O03 had to repeat it. O03 said this cost a week of a three-month visit.

Interpretation What it might mean

Time-to-first-booking appears to be driven partly by training logistics outside LabLoop. However, a way to log external or prior training for a trainer to verify might shorten access for experienced users, especially short-term visitors. Visibility of upcoming training sessions within LabLoop could also help, though no participant asked for that directly.

06

Visiting researchers cannot get into LabLoop until they have a university login

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by O03

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

O03 could not see the booking calendar for the first ten days of a three-month visit, because LabLoop required a university login and visitor paperwork was slow. O03 has not been told what happens to the account when the visit ends and wants to be able to download their booking history. O03 asked for a host-created guest account with an end date.

Interpretation What it might mean

Single-source, but visitors may be a recurring group: O02 mentions visiting researchers among their roughly eight new people a year. A host-sponsored guest account with an expiry date could address visitor onboarding and overlaps with the offboarding gap raised by O02 and O06.

07

Adding new users is one-at-a-time with no bulk import or bulk setup

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O06, O02

Evidence What participants said

O06 adds up to forty people at the start of term by typing each one in by hand from a graduate office spreadsheet. This takes most of two days, and O06 asked for spreadsheet upload. O02 asked for bulk actions: add a person, tick a role, done.

Interpretation What it might mean

Admin effort scales linearly with intake and peaks at term start, the same time new users are most likely to be confused. Bulk import combined with role assignment might cut admin time and reduce errors at the busiest point of the year.

08

Booking rules and instrument constraints are not communicated or enforced at booking time

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O04

Evidence What participants said

O01 did not know about an unwritten four-hour daily limit on the confocal, booked a full day, and received a frosty email. O01 expected LabLoop to tell them about such rules, or stop the booking, at the time of booking. O04 said new users do not realise the cell sorter needs thirty minutes of setup before their slot. Because LabLoop allows back-to-back bookings with no buffer, conflicts arise every couple of weeks, mostly with newer users.

Interpretation What it might mean

Local norms that experienced users know implicitly may be tripping up newcomers and causing social friction. Configurable per-instrument rules, such as maximum duration and setup buffers, shown or enforced during booking could prevent these conflicts without relying on word of mouth.

09

Human-provided onboarding guidance can fill the gaps the software leaves

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O05, O02

Evidence What participants said

O05 said onboarding went smoothly because their lab manager sent a short document with screenshots, a list of needed instruments, and who to ask for training. O05 believes they would otherwise have been as confused as some students. O02 prefers new users to come to a person, as a way of learning the lab culture.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

O04 Training coordinator says a human-first approach may work for a lab's own equipment but does not scale for a core serving around 120 new users a year.

Interpretation What it might mean

Onboarding quality may currently depend heavily on whether a particular lab manager provides good guidance, which leads to uneven experiences. LabLoop might support rather than replace human onboarding, for example by letting labs attach their own guidance or contacts. Needs may differ between small labs and shared cores.

10

Users are subscribed to notifications for every instrument by default, and the settings are hard to find

2 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O05

Evidence What participants said

O01 believes they are subscribed to every instrument by default and has not worked out how to turn off notifications for instruments they do not use. O05 received emails about unfamiliar instruments and eventually found the settings, which O05 described as three levels deep.

Interpretation What it might mean

Default-all subscriptions may create noise for new users in particular, which could cause them to ignore notifications that matter. Defaulting subscriptions to instruments a user is authorised for, or making settings easier to reach, might help.

11

Once users have access, the booking interface itself is seen as easy and clear

3 of 6 participants

Supported by O01, O03, O05

Evidence What participants said

O01 described booking as fine, mostly, with a clear calendar view. O03 understood booking in five minutes and said the only problem was getting in. O05 compared LabLoop favourably with a more expensive industry system, calling it simpler, clearer, and one of the more intuitive tools available.

Interpretation What it might mean

The core booking experience appears to be a strength. This suggests the onboarding problems sit around access, permissions, and accounts rather than in the booking interface, so effort may be better spent on getting users to their first successful booking.

12

Grant codes are often left blank by new users, which breaks billing

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by O06

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

O06 said new students often do not know which grant they are on and leave the code blank, so their usage cannot be billed properly at quarter end. O06 wants LabLoop to require a grant code at booking, picked from a list rather than typed.

Interpretation What it might mean

Single-source. A required, constrained grant-code field could improve billing data quality. However, forcing it at booking might add another barrier for new users who do not know their grant, so pre-assigning codes at account creation could be worth exploring.

13

No sync with personal calendars

1 of 6 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by O05

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

O05 uses Outlook and misses the automatic calendar sync their previous industry booking system provided. O05 named calendar sync as the one thing they would change.

Interpretation What it might mean

Single-source. This may matter more to users coming from industry or other institutions with richer tools. It is less tied to onboarding than the other findings, but it was this participant's top request.
