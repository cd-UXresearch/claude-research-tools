Findings

01

Mobile bookings can fail silently on poor connections, leaving users unsure whether a slot is theirs and leading to on-site conflicts

2 of 5 participants

Supported by M01, M02

Evidence What participants said

M01 tried to book the analyser from a field site with weak signal. The page loaded slowly, the confirm action spun without resolving, and they later found the booking had not saved and someone else had the instrument after they had driven back early. They asked for a clear confirmation or error message, or a text confirming the booking. M02, a technician, said that twice this term a booking made by phone did not save properly and two people turned up for the same slot. In one case the screenshot offered as proof showed the screen before confirmation. M02 said they had to settle these conflicts with a judgement call.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

M04 M04's one booking conflict was caused by a maintenance window added after booking, not by mobile use.

Interpretation What it might mean

The mobile flow may not clearly show whether a booking succeeded, failed or is still pending when the connection is unreliable. Users may then treat a pre-confirmation screen as a completed booking. Explicit success and failure states, and possibly a confirmation sent through a separate channel, could reduce lost slots and disputes that technicians currently have to settle by hand.

02

Time slots on the mobile calendar are too small to tap accurately, causing mis-bookings

2 of 5 participants

Supported by M01, M05

Evidence What participants said

M05 books only on their phone because they don't bring a laptop to the lab. They said they usually tap the wrong slot and have to cancel and rebook, and now zoom in a long way before tapping. They asked for bigger buttons and a 'book next free slot' option, and named bigger slots as their one change. M01 described the mobile calendar as the desktop calendar shrunk down and said they turn the phone sideways to cope.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

M04 M04 agreed the slots are small but said booking should not happen on a phone at all, and would rather effort went into the desktop version.

Interpretation What it might mean

The mobile calendar may be a scaled-down desktop layout rather than one designed for touch. This could hit hardest users who have no practical desktop access, such as people at the bench or in the field. Users disagree about whether mobile booking should be a priority, which suggests that improving it should not come at the expense of desktop users.

03

Booked equipment often sits unused, and there is no check-in to release no-show slots

2 of 5 participants

Supported by M02, M05

Evidence What participants said

M02 said people book the mass spec for whole mornings and don't turn up, and estimated, without having counted, that about one in five morning slots go unused. M02 had mounted a wall tablet hoping people would check in on it, but LabLoop has no check-in feature. They suggested tapping 'I'm here' within fifteen minutes or scanning a QR code on each instrument, and said a check-in record would also help with conflicts. M05 said the rotavap sometimes shows as booked with nobody there, but they don't feel able to use it in case the person returns.

Interpretation What it might mean

Without a way to confirm attendance, booked-but-empty slots cannot be released, and other users may hold back rather than risk taking someone's slot. A phone- or tablet-based check-in that releases slots automatically might recover lost instrument time. It could also create an attendance record that helps resolve disputes.

04

Email notifications are missed; some users want phone push notifications instead

2 of 5 participants

Supported by M01, M05

Evidence What participants said

M01 said they don't check email in the field but do see their lock screen, and would prefer push notifications. M05 said they get about ten LabLoop emails a day, mostly about other people's bookings, stopped reading them, and missed a cancellation of their own booking as a result.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

M04 M04 turned most notifications off and prefers to check the calendar directly.

Interpretation What it might mean

The volume and channel of notifications may be burying the messages users actually need, such as changes to their own bookings. Push notifications and more selective, user-relevant alerts could help. Since some users opt out entirely, notification preferences probably need to stay configurable.

05

Approving bookings on a phone is slow for PIs, which delays students' work

1 of 5 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by M03

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

M03 approves overnight runs on two instruments, usually by phone while travelling or in meetings. The approval email link leads to a full login, and the approvals page is hidden in a menu on mobile. Each approval takes two or three minutes. Delays mean students lose a day, and students have messaged M03's personal phone asking for approval. M03 asked for a one-tap approve button in the email or notification.

Interpretation What it might mean

The approval flow may be built around desktop navigation, while approvers mostly act from a phone between other commitments. One-tap approval from the notification itself could cut delays for students and approvers alike. This is single-source, so it is worth checking with other approvers.

06

Some users keep the phone for quick checks and do complex tasks on desktop, while others depend on mobile entirely

3 of 5 participants

Supported by M02, M03, M04

Evidence What participants said

M02 said the phone is fine for quick checks but goes to a desk to change maintenance windows because the phone version hides options. M04 plans and books the week on a laptop and uses the phone to check today's bookings or to cancel, which they found works fine. M03 uses a laptop for utilisation and cost reports and said they don't need those on mobile.

Contrasting views Participants who disagreed or saw it differently

M05 M05 books exclusively on their phone because there is nowhere safe for a laptop in the lab.

M01 M01 relies mainly on the phone because they spend half the week at remote field sites.

Interpretation What it might mean

How much people need mobile may depend on their role and setting. Desk-based users may need only lightweight mobile tasks, while bench and field users may need full booking on mobile. Prioritising mobile work around those who depend on it could avoid over-investing in mobile features that desk-based users don't want.

07

Desktop users want to see several instruments side by side to plan sequential bookings

1 of 5 participants⚑ Single source

Supported by M04

Single-participant finding. Only one person raised this. Treat it as a lead to explore, not a pattern.

Evidence What participants said

M04 often needs one instrument followed straight after by another, and has to flip between two calendars to line them up. Viewing several instruments side by side was the one change they asked for.

Interpretation What it might mean

Workflows that chain instruments may not be well supported by one-calendar-at-a-time views. A multi-instrument view could make planning easier for users who book in batches. This is single-source and comes from a user who explicitly prioritises desktop over mobile.
