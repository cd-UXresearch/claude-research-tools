## 1. The "not authorised" message gives no reason or next step, so new users get stuck and email the wrong people

**Support:** 3 of 6 participants (O01, O04, O06)

### Evidence

O01 saw "You are not authorised to book this resource" on every instrument with no reason given. O01 thought the account was broken and emailed the university IT helpdesk, which said LabLoop was not theirs, before the lab manager explained the training requirements. O01 asked for the message to say why and what to do. O04, the training coordinator for a shared core, said new users are confused by the message: some email O04, some email IT, and some ask a friend to book for them. O04 wants the message to link to training sign-up. O06, a department admin, receives many emails from confused new users who cannot book. O06 forwards them to lab managers or the core because O06's name is on the welcome email.

> "When it says I'm not authorised, tell me why and what to do. "You need confocal training, here's how to sign up." That would have saved me a lot of confused emails." (**O01**) ✅ verified

> "They're confused. They see the instrument, they try to book, they get "not authorised." Half of them email me, half email IT, and some just give up and ask a friend to book for them." (**O04**) ✅ verified

> "I get a lot of emails from confused new people who can't book anything. I usually have to forward them to their lab manager or the core." (**O06**) ✅ verified

### Contrasting views

- **O02:** Lab manager thinks it is fine that LabLoop does not explain access refusals. O02 sees onboarding as the lab's job and worries that a generic message could misdirect people away from the lab manager.
  > "I think it's fine that LabLoop doesn't explain everything. Onboarding is the lab's job, not the software's." ✅ verified
- **O05:** Saw the message once but was not confused, because a lab-provided document had already explained the training requirement.
  > "Once, for the flow cytometer. But I already knew from the document that I needed training for that, so it didn't surprise me." ✅ verified

### Interpretation

*The unexplained refusal may be generating misrouted support load across IT, admins, and core staff, as well as delays for new users. The disagreement suggests that a single generic message could clash with lab-specific processes. A configurable message, where each lab or core sets the reason, contact, or sign-up link, might satisfy both the core's need to scale and lab managers' wish to route people to a human.*

## 2. Granting instrument access after training is manual, instrument-by-instrument, and error-prone

**Support:** 2 of 6 participants (O02, O04)

### Evidence

O02 grants permissions one instrument at a time after training. This takes about twenty minutes per person, and when O02 forgets one, a user later finds they cannot book. O02 tracks training completion in a separate spreadsheet and asked for roles or bundles that grant and revoke permissions in one go. O04 said training records live in the university learning system, which LabLoop does not connect to, so O04 manually unlocks users after each session. This takes an hour or two per training day, and nobody gets unlocked when O04 is away. O04 once unlocked the wrong person with a similar name and reported that both untrained access and wrongly blocked trained users happen.

> "There's no way to say "this person has done the standard training package, give them these six instruments."" (**O02**) ✅ verified

> "Our training records live in the university's learning system. LabLoop doesn't know about them. So after every session, I sit with a list and manually tick people off in LabLoop. If I'm away, nobody gets unlocked." (**O04**) ✅ verified

> "Right now I keep that in a spreadsheet next to LabLoop, which is exactly the double handling I hoped to avoid." (**O02**) ✅ verified

### Interpretation

*The manual permission model may be a root cause of both the admin burden and some of the new-user access problems, including delays, missed permissions, and wrong unlocks. Role-based permission bundles and an integration with training records could reduce workload and errors. The wrong-person unlock suggests this also has a safety dimension, not just an efficiency one.*

## 4. Access barriers push new users into booking under other people's names

**Support:** 3 of 6 participants (O01, O03, O04)

### Evidence

While waiting for confocal training, O01 borrowed a second-year student's time on the machine and found this awkward. O03's host booked time under her own name while O03 had no account. As a result, others thought the host was using the microscope constantly, and problems appeared to be from her sessions. O04 reported that some new users who hit the "not authorised" message ask a friend to book for them.

> "I spent the first few weeks reading papers and borrowing a second-year's time on the machine, which felt a bit awkward." (**O01**) ✅ verified

> "My host booked time for me under her own name. Which worked, but then everyone thought she was using the microscope all the time, and when something went wrong it looked like it was her session." (**O03**) ✅ verified

> "some just give up and ask a friend to book for them" (**O04**) ✅ verified

### Interpretation

*Proxy bookings may undermine the accuracy of usage records, accountability for incidents, and possibly billing. They may also let untrained people onto instruments under someone else's name. Reducing time-to-access, or offering a sanctioned way to book on behalf of a trainee or guest, might reduce this behaviour.*

### Interpretation
